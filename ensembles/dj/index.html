DJ

The DJ, the corporate season's most common single booking.

Open format, on its own or carrying the late end after a band steps off, through the same sound system, run by the same team, on the same contract. On its own the DJ runs $1,500–2,800, and every figure around it is on the published rate card.

Most December parties split into two halves: dinner at conversation volume, then a floor that wants to move. The DJ is how the second half gets covered without a second vendor, a second load-in or a second invoice, and a duo through drinks and dinner with the DJ carrying the back half is the most common pairing of the season on the holiday party calendar. That season is decided August through October, and New Year's Eve books first.

The premium option

A live instrument over the set.

Bass or saxophone, played live on the floor over the DJ set. It is the difference between a playlist and a crowd watching something happen, and it holds a dance floor through the hours a band would have gone home.

It is priced on the rate card rather than treated as a favor, and quoted as its own line when you ask for it. On a corporate floor and a wedding floor it does the same work: the last two hours of the party stay something guests watch as well as dance to.

If the night should open with live players instead, Tejas Singh ladders from duo to quartet in front of the same late-night handoff, and the whole roster is on the roster page.

The practical part

A 6 by 4 ft booth on one circuit.

The DJ takes a 6 by 4 ft footprint, one dedicated 20-amp circuit, a 45-minute load-in and a 30-minute strike, with a PA sized to the venue included in the figure. A dedicated announcement microphone is live on its own channel from the end of sound check, so the program can borrow the system whenever it needs a voice.

On the music you get as much say as you want: build the list yourself, hand over a do-not-play list and leave the rest, or describe the crowd and the DJ reads it song by song. Requests from the floor get taken where they fit; a request that would empty the floor gets a straight answer and an alternative.

Open format means the set is built wider than any one list. The roster's live covers catalog, the songs the bands on this site play, is on the repertoire page, and footprints and power for every size are on the technical page.

DJ booking questions

The operational questions, from curfews to decibel caps, are on the full question list.

Can the DJ follow the band on the same contract?
Yes, and that is the most common shape of the corporate season. Once we are in the building the call-out is already paid, so the DJ added to a band booking bills at the hourly rate only, through the same sound system and on the same invoice. The handoff is planned against your run of show.
What does the DJ booking include?
Decks, booth and a PA sized to the venue, a dedicated announcement microphone on its own channel, load-in and strike, a certificate of insurance naming your venue, a W-9 and one itemised invoice. Sound is inside the published figure at every size, this one included.
How late can the DJ play?
As late as the venue allows. Added time bills at the same hourly rate in 30-minute increments, agreed on the night with whoever holds the budget, and there is no overtime rate. Venue curfews and decibel caps are honoured as written and built into the plan at contract.
What is the DJ plus live instrument option?
A live bass or saxophone played on the floor over the set. It reads as a crowd watching something happen rather than a playlist, and it holds a dance floor late. It is a named option priced on the rate card, quoted as its own line when you ask for it.
Do you take requests on the night?
From you, always, and from guests where they fit. A do-not-play list is the part worth spending time on before the event. A request that would empty the floor gets a straight answer and an alternative rather than a yes and a bad twenty minutes.
