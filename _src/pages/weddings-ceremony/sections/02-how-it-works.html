How it works

Four parts, in this order.

Ceremony music is the part of a wedding almost nobody has planned before, and it is simpler than it looks. There are four parts, three of them are songs you choose, and the fourth is not a song at all.

The prelude
What plays while guests arrive and take their seats, usually twenty to thirty minutes before the start. It has no set list and no cue. It runs continuously at a volume people can greet each other over, and it ends when the officiant is in place. Its whole job is to keep a waiting stretch from feeling like a delay, and it is the part couples most often leave out and most often regret leaving out.
The processional
One or two pieces: often one for the wedding party and a second for the entrance everybody stood up for. This is where a live player earns the booking, because an aisle takes as long as it takes and a recording does not know that. The piece stretches, the last phrase lands on arrival, and the guests never hear a fade.
The interlude
Optional, and only if the ceremony has a still moment in it: a reading, a unity candle or sand, a signing, a communion. Guests are seated with nothing to look at, so a single piece under it holds the space. This is also the one place in a ceremony where a sung song works.
The recessional
One piece, faster than everything before it, starting the moment the couple turns around. It carries them out and then keeps going while the guest rows empty, which usually means playing a few minutes longer than the song itself.

How many songs, and how long. Three named pieces plus the prelude, which is the answer to a question people usually ask as a number. Total playing time comes to about 45 to 60 minutes: twenty to thirty minutes of prelude, then the ceremony itself, which runs twenty to thirty minutes for a civil or non-denominational service and longer for a religious one. Book the hour, not the twelve minutes of named songs inside it.
