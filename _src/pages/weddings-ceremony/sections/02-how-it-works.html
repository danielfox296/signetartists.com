How it works

The four parts of ceremony music, in order.

Ceremony music is the part of a wedding most couples have never planned before, and it's simpler than it looks. There are four parts, and three of them are songs you choose.

The prelude
What plays while guests arrive and take their seats, usually twenty to thirty minutes before the start. It has no set list and no cue. It runs continuously at a volume people can greet each other over, and it ends when the officiant is in place. It keeps the wait from feeling like a delay, and it's the part couples most often leave out.
The processional
One or two pieces: often one for the wedding party and a second for the entrance everybody stood up for. This is where a live musician makes the difference. An aisle takes as long as it takes, so the piece stretches, the last phrase lands on arrival, and the guests never hear a fade.
The interlude
Optional, and only if the ceremony has a still moment in it: a reading, a unity candle or sand, a signing, a communion. Guests are seated with nothing to look at, so a single piece played under it fills the silence. This is also the one place in a ceremony where a sung song works.
The recessional
One piece, faster than everything before it, starting the moment the couple turns around. It carries them out and then keeps going while the guest rows empty, which usually means playing a few minutes longer than the song itself.

How many songs, and how long. Three named pieces plus the prelude. Total playing time usually comes to about 45 to 60 minutes: twenty to thirty minutes of prelude, then the ceremony itself, which runs twenty to thirty minutes for a civil or non-denominational service and longer for a religious one. Booking the hour covers all of it.
