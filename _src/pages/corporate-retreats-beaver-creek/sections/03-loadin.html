What on-mountain load-in genuinely means.

Some Beaver Creek venues are reached over snow for half the year, and gear travels the way the venue says it travels: by sleigh, snowcat or gondola in winter, by vehicle in summer. Which of those it is, when the route runs, and where cases wait through dinner are questions we put to the venue in writing before the day. The answers decide what we bring and when it moves, and none of them belongs at the base at four o'clock on the day.

The village runs a different version of the same question. The core is pedestrian and terraced, escalators carry guests between the parking structures and the plaza, and gear takes the dock and the service route instead, on a window the hotel schedules. The distance from dock to floor gets measured into the plan in advance.

Power is one or two dedicated 20-amp circuits depending on the size of the band, and the footprint runs from 6 by 4 feet for a solo to 14 by 10 for a quartet, all published by size. The village sits near 8,100 feet, which is why a longer sound check goes into the run of show, with a retune between sets. Beaver Creek's own noise cap is one of the figures our mountain corridor page names as unconfirmed rather than guessed at: send us the venue and we get the number in writing before you have to.
