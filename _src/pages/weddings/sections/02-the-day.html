The day

Three moments, three different jobs.

Couples usually start by asking what band to book, and the more useful question is which parts of the day need live players at all. These three are not interchangeable, and the act that is right for one of them is wrong for the next.

The ceremony
The one stretch of the day with a whole guest list paying attention to it, so the music is exposed and the timing has to be exact. A guitarist plays the guests in, plays the processional, holds under the ring exchange if the couple wants it, and plays the recessional. How the parts are ordered, how many songs each one needs and how long it all runs are on the ceremony page, along with acoustic guitar and Spanish guitar as the two versions of the same booking.
The cocktail hour
The turn of the day. The couple leaves for photographs, guests stand with a first drink deciding what kind of night this is, and the venue may be turning ceremony seating into dinner tables behind a wall. A duo at 65 to 72 dBA covers the whole hour without asking for attention. What plays, how many songs, whether anyone notices and whether a duo is enough for seventy guests are all answered on the cocktail hour page.
The reception
A floor that has to fill and stay full for hours, and that is a DJ's job rather than a duo's. An open-format DJ reads the crowd from the first dance to the last song, on the same contract and through the same sound system the earlier music used. Where a wedding wants live players for dinner and a DJ after it, that is one booking rather than two, and the drive, the load-in and the sound system are paid for once.
