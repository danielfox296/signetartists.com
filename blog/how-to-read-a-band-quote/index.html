How to read a band quote

A quote is one number sitting on top of six or seven decisions. The number is the last thing to look at. Here is the rest of it, in the order it matters.

Signet Artists · August 24, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways
  • Two quotes are only comparable once they assume the same set count, the same sound arrangement and the same travel terms. Normalise first, compare second.
  • Sound is the line that most often separates two totals that otherwise look alike. Our sound support is included in the number at every size.
  • Travel is geography, and it belongs on its own line: a short one to the Front Range, a longer one to the I-70 corridor, and travel plus a night's lodging to the far resorts.
  • The four items that cost nothing on a quote and cost a week when they're missing: a certificate of insurance, a W-9, one itemised invoice and a personal contact.

A band quote is one number sitting on top of six or seven decisions somebody already made for you. Read from the number upward and you learn almost nothing, because two totals a few hundred dollars apart can cover very different evenings.

This is what each decision is, and how to see it on paper. It works on our quote and it works on anyone else’s.

Start with how the fee is built #

Most band pricing has two parts. One part covers the evening the band commits to regardless of how long they play: travel, load-in, setup, sound check, strike, load-out and production. The other part covers performance time by the hour.

Those are usually called the call-out and the hourly rate, and what live music costs for a corporate event works through what the market charges for each configuration. If a quote shows one flat figure, ask which part is which, and the rest of the conversation gets easier.

Personnel, which moves the number most #

Ensemble size is the single largest driver of a band’s price, and it’s the line most often described in words rather than in a count. “Full band” isn’t a number. Four people and six people are different budgets and different footprints.

Get the head count in writing, then check it against the space. A venue that seats 120 and a venue that seats 400 want different sizes, and what a band needs from the room covers the footprint each one takes.

Sound, which is where two quotes usually part ways #

A quote either includes a system sized to your venue, or it assumes the venue is providing one, or it assumes you’ll hire an engineer separately. All three are legitimate arrangements and they produce very different totals.

Sound support that’s the right size for the venue is included in our numbers at every size, along with continuous audio during breaks. Stage lighting and a dedicated engineer are optional and quoted on their own line, so they’re visible before you agree to them.

When you compare two quotes, this is the first thing to level. Put both on the same sound assumption and the gap between them will often close or reverse.

Time, and what happens when the night runs long #

Check three things: how many sets, how long each one is, and what an extra half hour costs late in the night.

Our extra time runs at the same hourly figure in 30-minute increments, with no overtime rate. Hour five costs what hour four cost. That’s worth asking about wherever you’re reading, because a night that runs long is common and the terms for it are usually decided in advance.

Travel and lodging, which are geography #

Distance is a real cost and it belongs on the page before you ask. Our travel is itemised by corridor: a short line for the Front Range, meaning Boulder, Colorado Springs and Fort Collins. A longer one for the I-70 corridor, meaning Vail, Beaver Creek, Breckenridge and Winter Park. For the far resorts, Aspen, Telluride, Steamboat and Crested Butte, it’s travel plus a night’s lodging billed at cost, because a band that packed up late isn’t driving that road home.

When you read any quote for a mountain date, find where the distance is accounted for, and ask if it isn’t on its own line.

The paperwork lines #

Four items cost nothing on a quote and cost a week when they’re missing: a certificate of insurance, a W-9, one itemised invoice and a personal contact for the night.

All four are included in our numbers. Wherever you’re reading, confirm they exist before the deposit, because the week of the event is when your venue will ask for the first one.

Now compare the two quotes #

Normalise before you total. Put both quotes on the same set count, the same sound arrangement and the same travel terms, and add the missing pieces to whichever one is missing them. Only then look at the two figures.

Most of the time the exercise doesn’t change which band you want. It changes what you thought you were choosing between, which is the more useful outcome for the person who has to defend the line item.
