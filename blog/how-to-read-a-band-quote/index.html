How to read a band quote

A quote is one number sitting on top of six or seven decisions. The number is the last thing to look at. Here is the rest of it, in the order it matters.

Signet Artists · August 24, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Two quotes are only comparable once they assume the same set count, the same sound arrangement and the same travel terms. Normalise first, compare second.
  • Sound is the line that most often separates two totals that otherwise look alike. Ours is inside the number at every size.
  • Travel is geography, not a surcharge: $300 to the Front Range, $600 to the I-70 corridor, $600 plus lodging at cost to the far resorts.
  • The four items that cost nothing on a quote and cost a week when they are missing: a certificate of insurance, a W-9, one itemised invoice and a named contact.

A band quote is one number sitting on top of six or seven decisions somebody already made for you. Read from the number upward and you learn almost nothing, because two totals a few hundred dollars apart can cover genuinely different evenings.

This is what each decision is, and how to see it on paper. It works on our quote and it works on anyone else’s.

Start with the shape of the fee #

Most band pricing has two parts. One part covers the evening the band commits to regardless of how long they play: travel, load-in, setup, sound check, strike, load-out and production. The other part covers performance time by the hour.

We call those the call-out and the hourly rate, and what live music costs for a corporate event works through the arithmetic with totals. The reason the split matters when you are reading someone else’s paper is that a quote showing one flat figure has made both decisions and shown you neither. Ask which part is which and the rest of the conversation gets easier.

Personnel, which moves the number most #

Ensemble size is the single largest driver of a band’s price, and it is the line most often described in words rather than in a count. “Full band” is not a number. Four people and six people are different budgets and different rooms.

Get the head count in writing, then check it against the space. A room that seats 120 and a room that seats 400 want different sizes, and what a band needs from the room covers the footprint each one takes.

Sound, which is where two quotes usually part ways #

This is the item worth your attention. A quote either includes a system sized to your venue, or it assumes the venue is providing one, or it assumes you will hire an engineer separately. All three are legitimate arrangements and they produce very different totals.

Sound sized to the venue is inside our numbers at every size, along with continuous audio during breaks. Stage lighting and a dedicated engineer are optional and quoted on their own line, so they are visible before you agree to them rather than after.

When you compare two quotes, this is the first thing to level. Put both on the same sound assumption and the gap between them will often close or reverse.

Time, and what happens when the night runs long #

Check three things: how many sets, how long each one is, and what an extra half hour costs at eleven o’clock.

Ours is the same hourly figure in 30-minute increments, with no overtime rate. Hour five costs what hour four cost. That is worth asking about wherever you are reading, because a night that runs long is common and the terms for it are usually decided in advance whether or not anyone told you.

Travel and lodging, which are geography #

Distance is a real cost and it belongs on the page before you ask. Ours is published by corridor: $300 for the Front Range, meaning Boulder, Colorado Springs and Fort Collins. $600 for the I-70 corridor, meaning Vail, Beaver Creek, Breckenridge and Winter Park. $600 for the far resorts, Aspen, Telluride, Steamboat and Crested Butte, with lodging billed at cost as its own line on the contract.

Resort dates sit in their own column on our rate card rather than arriving as a percentage later. When you read any quote for a mountain date, find where distance is accounted for. It is in there somewhere.

The paperwork lines #

Four items cost nothing on a quote and cost a week when they are missing: a certificate of insurance naming your venue, a W-9, one itemised invoice and a named contact for the night given to you before the event.

All four are included in our numbers. Wherever you are reading, confirm they exist before the deposit rather than during the week of the event, because that is the week your venue will ask for the first one.

Now compare the two quotes #

Normalise before you total. Put both quotes on the same set count, the same sound arrangement and the same travel terms, and add the missing pieces to whichever one is missing them. Only then look at the two figures.

Most of the time the exercise does not change which band you want. It changes what you thought you were choosing between, which is the more useful outcome for the person who has to defend the line item.
