Playing around a program

A gala runs on a document with eleven things in a fixed order. What the band does around remarks, an award, an auction and a video package, and who calls each one.

Signet Artists · August 5, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • A corporate evening runs on a run of show. The band's job is scheduled against that document, the same as catering and AV.
  • The music ends before the speaker reaches the microphone, so nobody stands at the side of the stage waiting for a song to end.
  • Every program moment needs one person who calls it, agreed at sound check.
  • Dinner volume is a decision somebody makes in advance and writes into the run of show.

A wedding reception has a structure most of the guests already know. People can feel what comes next even when they haven’t seen the timeline.

A gala doesn’t work that way. There’s a document, usually called the run of show, and it exists because eleven things have to happen in a specific order in front of three hundred people who are also eating. The chief executive speaks. An award goes out to four recipients whose names are on a card somebody printed that afternoon. A video plays. An auctioneer works the crowd for eighteen minutes. Somewhere in there, the dancing is supposed to start.

The band works from the run of show #

We plan the sets against the run of show, and the transitions get most of that attention, because transitions are where an evening breaks.

What that buys you is specific. The bandleader knows the award presentation follows the salad course rather than the entrée, so the set before it is built to end there. When the program slips twelve minutes, whoever holds the run of show can tell the bandleader directly.

Remarks #

The music ends before the speaker reaches the microphone. That sounds like a small distinction until you’ve watched the other version, where the band plays out the last eight bars of a song while your chief executive stands at the side of the stage with a card in their hand, waiting, in front of everybody.

So the band ends the song early and the crowd goes quiet before the walk starts. Then there’s silence through the remarks. Then the music comes back when the applause starts, which is a cue the band can hear without anybody signalling it.

An award #

An award presentation is a sequence of short cues rather than one long one, and the number of cues equals the number of recipients. Each one wants a few bars to walk in on and a clean ending under the applause.

The thing that makes this work is agreeing, at sound check, on one person who signals the next recipient. Usually that’s the emcee. Occasionally it’s whoever is running the deck, because the name on the screen changes before the person moves. What matters is that it’s decided at sound check.

An auction is a different problem #

An auctioneer needs the crowd’s whole attention and complete silence, and then needs it again eleven times. That part is straightforward. Everybody understands that you don’t play under bidding.

The harder part comes after. A crowd that has just spent twenty minutes concentrating, bidding against colleagues and doing arithmetic about money comes out of it quiet and slightly tired, having spent its attention on something other than the party. Starting a dance set into that quiet is a different job from starting one after dinner.

The band handles it by building the energy back up. That’s a judgement made in the moment, by somebody watching the floor, which is why the bandleader reads the set song by song.

The video package and the audio handoff #

A video package is the moment where two sound systems are in the same space and only one of them should be making noise. The band mutes, house audio takes over, and the handoff happens on a cue.

This is the single most common place for an evening to go audibly wrong, and the cause is almost always that the band and the AV vendor never spoke. They’re usually two different companies who met that afternoon. Settle it before doors: whose desk the video audio runs through, who mutes what, and who gives the cue. It takes four minutes at sound check and it removes that problem entirely.

Volume is a program element #

Dinner and networking sit at conversation volume, which means people at a round table of ten can talk to each other without leaning in. The night gets loud later, when loud is the point.

That shift is scheduled, the same as the courses are. Put it in the run of show, because “the band was too loud” is nearly always a description of the wrong volume at the wrong hour. If your event has a window where a sponsor needs to be able to hold a conversation, put it in the document and we’ll honour it.

What to send, and when

Send the run of show a week out, in whatever state it’s in. It will change, and that’s fine. Then send the names and the order for any walk-ons as soon as they exist, the AV vendor’s contact, and the venue’s load-in instructions. If there’s a song that means something to your organisation, send that too. The first learned song is included.

What this looks like when it works #

Nobody at the party notices any of it. The chief executive walks into silence. The award recipients each get their eight bars. The auctioneer gets silence through the bidding, and the music comes back when the bidding ends. The video plays without a squeal of two systems arguing.

Then the band starts the dance set and three hundred people who have been sitting through dinner get up at once, and for the rest of the night the only thing anybody remembers about the program is that it went well.
