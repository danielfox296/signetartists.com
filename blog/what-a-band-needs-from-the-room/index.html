What a band needs from the venue

Stage footprint, power, load-in and the paperwork your venue and your finance team will both ask for. Including why published stage-size guidance runs to more than double what our own bands ask for.

Signet Artists · August 5, 2026 · 6 min read

Key takeaways
  • Stage-rental guidance for a seven to ten-piece band runs to 24 ft by 24 ft. The largest band we sell, a quartet, asks for 14 ft by 10 ft and will play on 12 ft by 8 ft.
  • Power is the requirement venues most often get wrong. A band needs its own dedicated circuits, within reach of the stage.
  • Ask the band for its rider before you promise the venue anything. The rider is the figure that binds.
  • Insurance, a W-9 and one itemised invoice are a procurement gate with a queue behind each one, and they should arrive with the contract.

You booked the band. A week later the venue sends a form with four questions on it, and three of them are about things you have never had a reason to think about. How much stage. How much power. What time the truck arrives. There is usually a fourth box asking for a certificate of insurance, and that one has a deadline.

None of these are hard questions. They are just questions somebody has to actually ask the band, and the failure mode is that nobody does until the week of, at which point the answer has consequences for a floor plan that was signed off in June.

How much stage does a band need? #

Search this and you will land on stage-rental companies, who publish recommendations by band size. Stage Rental puts a seven to ten-piece band on 20 ft by 24 ft at the small end and 24 ft by 24 ft in the middle, all at 4 ft of height.

Then look at what the bands you would actually book here ask for. Our footprints are published, size by size, on the technical page: the ask where the band is comfortable, and beside it the smallest stage the band will play on.

The gap is the useful part. Take the quartet, the largest band on our card. The rental company’s middle recommendation for a band that size works out to 320 square feet. The quartet asks for 140 and will play on 96.

Both figures are honest, and they answer different questions. The rental company is sizing for sight lines, for audiences who need to see over seated tables, and for the version of the event where the stage is a rented structure in a venue that has none. The band is describing the smallest footprint where nobody hits anybody. In a hotel ballroom with a built-in riser and eleven round tables you would like to keep, the difference between those two numbers is a couple of those tables.

So the number that binds is the band’s, and it should come from the band rather than from an article. Ask for the rider before you promise the venue a footprint.

Power, which is where it usually goes wrong #

The John Parker Band’s published requirement is two standard 20-amp, 120-volt circuits within 25 feet of the stage, with 15 amps as the floor. That shape of requirement is typical, and there are two words in it doing most of the work.

Circuits. Two outlets on the same wall are very often the same circuit, which means the requirement gets ticked off on paper while the band still has one circuit. When that fails it fails at the worst possible moment, because everything switches on at once, and that moment is the downbeat of the first song.

Within 25 feet. Distance is a hard constraint. A band running power from across a ballroom is running cable across a path that guests and servers will walk for four hours.

The person who can answer both of these is the venue’s engineer or head of operations. Your sales contact will forward the question to them anyway, so start there, and ask early enough that the answer can still change something.

Load-in #

Load-in gets coordinated with the venue in advance, and the questions are always the same ones: dock times, elevator windows, how far the door is from the stage, where the cases sit during dinner, and what time the space has to be clear at the end.

That last one is worth pinning down before you sign anything. Strike takes as long as it takes, and it starts after the last song, so a hard curfew on the venue sets the time the music has to end.

Mountain venues #

Properties in the corridor run on their own rules, and those rules differ by property rather than by town. Decibel limits and curfews are set by the venue, written into your contract with them, and honoured as written. Get them in writing from the property and send them to the band. Anybody who quotes you a number for a mountain venue without having read that venue’s own terms is guessing.

Travel is its own line. Vail, Beaver Creek, Breckenridge and Winter Park add $600. Aspen, Telluride, Steamboat and Crested Butte add $600 plus lodging at cost. Lodging is billed as what it actually cost, which in that corridor is a number worth confirming before you commit to a date in February.

The paperwork your finance team will ask for #

This is the part that stops bookings, and it stops them late. A venue will not let a vendor load in without a certificate of insurance, and accounts payable will not pay an invoice that arrives without a W-9 on file.

Three documents settle it:

  • A certificate of insurance naming your venue as additional insured. The venue is checking that document for its own name on it.
  • A W-9, before accounts payable asks for it.
  • One itemised invoice, with everything on separate lines, in a form finance will accept the first time.

Ours arrive in the same email as the contract, and net-30 is available on approval. The reason to send them unprompted is that every one of them is a gate somebody else controls, and each gate has a queue.

The four things to ask the band first

Footprint and height. Circuits and distance. Load-in and strike windows. Certificate of insurance with your venue named. If a band cannot answer those four in a single email, that is the useful signal, and it arrives early enough to matter.

Why any of this ends up being your problem #

The venue asks you. You are the contract holder, so the form comes to your inbox with a date on it.

The version of this that works is the one where you forward the question and get an answer the same day from somebody who has loaded into that building before. Every booking here comes with a named contact for the night, given to you before the event. The reason they exist is this, weeks before anybody plays a note.
