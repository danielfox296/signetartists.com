Is live music better than recorded?

What researchers found when they compared musicians playing in the same space with a recording played back, and what live music hasn't been shown to do.

Signet Artists · October 3, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • In the studies we found, people moved more and their bodies responded more when the musicians were playing in the same space.
  • People at a concert in person felt more connected to the rest of the audience than people watching it on a livestream.
  • Live music hasn't been shown to make the music itself more moving, and we found no study comparing live and recorded music at a wedding or a party.

Hearing a song you know played by people standing a few feet away feels different from hearing it through a speaker. Most of us have felt that at a wedding or a bar, even with a song we’ve heard a hundred times. If you’re deciding between live musicians and a playlist for your event, you might wonder whether that difference is real or just in your head. Researchers have wondered the same thing, and they’ve been able to measure some of it.

Why does live music feel different? #

Part of it may be that you can see the people playing. A 2012 analysis combined the results of 15 studies, with 1,298 people in them. When people could see the performers as well as hear them, they rated the music more highly, and the difference was a medium-sized one. The catch is that those studies compared seeing the musicians with only hearing them, and most of them used video.

Your body seems to respond too. In a 2016 study, 37 people heard pianists play live and then, about ten weeks later, heard recordings of the same pianists in the same place. Their heart rates followed the tempo of the music only when it was live. Everyone heard the live version first, though, so the order may have played a part.

In a 2024 study, 27 people lay in a brain scanner while a pianist played live for them, and afterwards they heard recordings. The live playing produced a stronger response in the part of the brain that handles emotion. But the pianist was adjusting the playing to a readout of the listener’s brain, inside a scanner, and that’s a long way from a party. If you’re curious about that side of it, here’s a longer read on the different ways music makes people feel something.

Do people move more to live music? #

They did in a 2019 study. One audience watched a rock musician play his new album live, and another audience heard the same album played back in the same 106-seat hall, through the same sound system. The researchers tracked 49 people with motion capture, and heads moved faster at the live show. That was only one show of each kind.

Does live music bring people together? #

In 2023, researchers studied a string quartet concert that 91 people attended in person and 32 watched on a livestream. The people in the hall felt more connected to the rest of the audience than the people watching online did, though they didn’t feel any more connected to the musicians. That was a single concert.

Another 2023 study measured 132 audience members at three chamber-music concerts. Their heart rates and breathing moved in time with one another, and more so when they said they felt moved. There was no recorded music to compare with in that one, so we can’t tell whether a recording would do the same.

What live music has not been shown to do #

In that string quartet study, how moved people felt depended on which piece was being played, and it made no difference whether they were in the hall or watching the stream. So live music hasn’t been shown to make the music itself more moving.

A few popular claims go further than the research does. You may have read that going to a gig every two weeks adds nine years to your life. That came from a 2018 marketing study paid for by a company that owns concert venues, and it was never published as research. Another one is that concerts lower your stress hormones. The study behind that measured people’s saliva before and after two concerts and had no comparison group, and the stress hormone cortisol goes down over the course of any evening anyway.

There’s one long-term study on going out and living longer. Researchers followed 6,710 English adults over 50 for 14 years, and those who went to arts events every few months or more were 31% less likely to die in that time. It counted concerts together with museums, galleries and theater, though, and it can’t show that going out caused the difference.

We also found no study comparing live musicians with recorded music at a wedding or a party, and none comparing an acoustic act with an amplified one.

What this means for your event #

Since seeing the musicians seems to matter, it can help to set them up where most of your guests can see them.
