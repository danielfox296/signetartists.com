Sing-along songs for seniors

Songs that work for a sing-along with older adults, how to choose more for your residents, and what trials of group singing found.

Signet Artists · October 4, 2026 · 5 min read

Key takeaways
  • Songs released when people were about 10 to 30 tend to feel the most personal. For a resident who's 80 in 2026, that's roughly 1956 to 1976.
  • In a trial in San Francisco senior centers, six months of a weekly choir lowered loneliness and raised interest in life more than waiting did, though it didn't change memory, strength or symptoms of depression.
  • The researchers studied people who sang in a group week after week, and none of the studies took place in memory care.

A song starts in the common area, and one table at a time, people join in until most of the residents are singing the same chorus. If you’re planning a sing-along for older adults, picking songs everyone already knows is the place to start.

Which songs work for a senior sing-along? #

These all come from our own song list and from our senior living sets, grouped by kind.

The standards. Our musicians play Autumn Leaves, All of Me, Someday My Prince Will Come and The Girl from Ipanema for residents.

The Beatles. Our Beatles sing-along plays Hey Jude, Let It Be, Yesterday, Blackbird, Come Together, Eleanor Rigby, A Hard Day’s Night, Something, While My Guitar Gently Weeps, Rocky Raccoon and Mother Nature’s Son.

Motown and soul. Our song list has My Girl, Respect, Lean on Me, Stand By Me and (Sittin’ On) The Dock of the Bay.

The sixties and seventies. Sweet Caroline, Brown Eyed Girl, Bad Moon Rising, Have You Ever Seen the Rain and Take It Easy are all on the song list, too.

Country and folk. There’s also Ring of Fire, Thank God I’m a Country Boy and Take Me Home, Country Roads.

How do you choose songs for your residents? #

It helps to start with the years they were young. In a 2024 study of 112 older adults rating songs from the 1950s to the 1980s, the songs that felt most personal were the ones released when the listener was between about 10 and 30. Researchers in a larger 2025 study, of 1,891 people, put the peak for personally meaningful songs at age 17.

By our arithmetic, for a resident who’s 80 in 2026, those years are roughly 1956 to 1976, and for a resident who’s 90 they’re roughly 1946 to 1966. It’s also worth asking residents and their families which songs they love, since any one person may have favorites from a different decade.

How a song is sung seems to matter as well. Researchers in the north of England counted how much people sang along to 1,054 songs across 30 nights in five venues, and found that people sang along more to songs sung in a full voice with clear words. Those were nights out rather than afternoons in a senior community, but it’s still a good reason to have someone leading the singing who sings the words out clearly.

Is singing together good for older adults? #

In two trials of weekly singing groups for older adults, a few things improved, though not everything the researchers were looking for.

In San Francisco, 12 senior centers were assigned at random either to start a choir straight away or to wait six months. In all, 390 people took part, with an average age of 71, and the choirs met weekly for 90 minutes with professional choir directors. After six months, loneliness had fallen and interest in life had risen more in the choir group than in the centers that waited. The researchers found no change in thinking and memory, physical strength or symptoms of depression, which were the things the trial had mainly set out to improve.

In England, 258 people aged 60 and over were assigned at random to community singing groups or to carry on as usual. At six months, the singers scored a little better on a measure of mental wellbeing. Their gains had been bigger at three months and had partly faded by six.

Singing also seemed to bring a new group together faster in a 2015 British study. Researchers followed 135 adults, aged 18 to 83, in new adult-education classes, and the people in singing classes felt closer to their classmates after the first month than the people in craft and writing classes did. The other classes caught up by the third month.

The catch is that all three looked at people who sang in a group week after week, rather than on a single afternoon, and none of them was done in memory care.

What a sing-along with us looks like #

Our Beatles sing-along, Your Bird Can Sing, is two musicians who play the Beatles and lead the singing. Everyone gets a lyric booklet, so a resident who doesn’t remember the second verse can read along, and anyone who’d rather sit and listen is welcome to. It’s about as loud as a conversation, and in a small space it can play with no amplification at all. In memory care, the same duo usually plays a shorter, quieter set for a smaller group.

If your residents would rather sing Motown, folk and country songs, a singer with a guitar can lead those instead. Either one can come once, or on a standing afternoon every month or every other week, with the set changing from visit to visit.
