How does music affect people at a party?

What research has found about music and how people behave at events, and the popular claims the research doesn't support.

Signet Artists · October 3, 2026 · 4 min read

Key takeaways
  • The volume of the music has a lot to do with how easily guests can talk, and crowds tend to move together most on songs they know.
  • People seem to move more when the musicians are in the same space, and to feel more connected to each other.
  • Several popular claims about music, like the idea that going to gigs adds years to your life, fall apart when you check where they came from.

Pretty much every party has music playing. Usually it’s a playlist somebody put together or whatever the venue already had on, and nobody picked it for what it might do to the guests. There’s a longer essay on how every party already has a soundtrack if you’re curious, and here’s a quick tour of what researchers have found so far.

What the research has found #

The volume of the music has a lot to do with who can talk to whom. Two people at arm’s length can keep a conversation going with up to about 71 decibels of sound around them, and one large banquet, measured in three halls, ran at 83 to 87 decibels, well past that. Learn more about how loud background music should be.

On the dance floor, crowds seem to move together most on songs they know, at tempos near walking pace. At one concert, bass too low for people to hear made them move about 12% more. Find out more about how to get people to dance at a wedding.

A guest’s age makes a difference too. The songs people have the most memories of are usually from their teens, and younger guests also respond to their parents’ music. Check out our post on party music for all ages for what that means for a song list.

In the few studies there are, people moved more when the music was live, and the audience felt more connected to each other. Read more about whether live music is better than recorded.

In lab studies, music seems to speed up drinking compared with silence. The famous finding that louder music means more drinking rests on one small study from a lab whose work is in question. Learn more about whether loud music makes people drink more.

Memory care and sound baths #

In one study, residents with dementia were positively engaged 69% of the time during live music, 25% with recorded music and 12.5% in silence. Learn more about live music for memory care residents, including what it hasn’t been shown to do.

People report feeling calmer after a sound bath, and the evidence for any health claim is thin. Find out more about whether sound baths work.

More questions we looked into #

People tend to remember the high point and the ending of an experience more than how long it lasted, which is a good reason to plan the last song of the night. In one count of 1,054 songs, crowds sang along more later in the night, and you can read more about why people sing along.

In two studies, songs brought back memories more strongly than television shows or photographs of famous faces did. Find out more about why music brings back memories. In lab tastings, loud noise made food taste less sweet, and there’s more on that in whether music makes food taste better.

Singing together seems to help people feel close quickly, though it hasn’t been shown to make a team work together better. We wrote about that in karaoke team building. In restaurants, music changed how long guests stayed more reliably than what they spent. Learn more about why restaurants have live music.

For older listeners, check out what music 80 year olds listen to, sing-along songs for seniors and why people with dementia remember music. And if you’re arranging a service, we looked at why people play music at funerals.

Claims we checked and dropped #

You’ll hear these a lot, but when we looked into them, the evidence didn’t back them up.

How we read the research #

For every claim we write about, we say what was found, how big the effect was, who was studied and where, and what the catch is (one night, a small group, no comparison group). Some findings rest on many studies and some rest on one small one, and we think you should know which is which before you plan around them.

As far as we can tell, some questions haven’t been studied at all. Nobody has measured music at a charity auction, compared an acoustic act with an amplified one, or counted how much strangers talk at a party with and without music. When a question like that comes up, we’ll tell you the research isn’t there yet.
