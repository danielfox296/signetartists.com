Company offsite ideas in Colorado for remote teams

The one week a year a distributed company is in the same place: how remote teams plan the offsite, where they go in Colorado, and seven evenings that do the work the agenda cannot.

Signet Artists · September 14, 2026 · 9 min read

Key takeaways
  • The people who organise company offsites are overwhelmingly remote or hybrid themselves: 91 percent of offsite organisers work that way, and 57 percent of fully remote companies call the offsite essential to the business.
  • Most companies gather once or twice a year for a few days, and September is the busiest month. Winter offsites are the growth segment, which in Colorado means a ski-town evening.
  • The agenda is meetings and team building by day. The evenings are where a team that has only met on video becomes a team, and the evening is the part the agenda usually leaves blank.
  • A third of planners now report more demand for locally flavored events, and Colorado is a place that can deliver one without a theme.

For a company with no office, the offsite is the office. It’s the one week a year, sometimes two, when people who have worked together for three years shake hands, and everything the company believes about its culture has to survive contact with a hotel ballroom and a dinner reservation for sixty.

The numbers say how common that week has become. 91 percent of the people organising company offsites work in a fully remote or hybrid arrangement themselves, 57 percent of fully remote companies say the offsite is essential to the success of the business, and 70 percent of companies hold one or two a year. The average offsite runs about four and a half days and three and a half nights, on weekdays, with team building named as the primary objective by 85 percent of companies. Behind those figures sits the workforce itself: 52 percent of remote-capable employees in the United States work hybrid and 26 percent work exclusively remote, so the company that has to fly everyone in to see each other isn’t the exception anymore.

This post is for the person planning that week in Colorado: what the agenda usually holds, where remote teams go here, and seven evenings that do the part of the job the working sessions can’t.

What a company offsite agenda usually holds #

The pattern is consistent across the reports and the planners’ own threads. Nearly every group has meeting space at the accommodation. Meals are at the accommodation too, with 92 percent of groups taking three or more there. The average stay has two team-building activities in it. It runs on weekdays, because a remote company’s offsite is work, and September is the single most popular month, holding 18 percent of the year’s offsites, with December-to-March bookings up 21 percent year on year. Budgets have moved the same direction: 59 percent of companies have increased what they spend on offsites since 2019.

The advice that recurs when engineers and managers ask each other about offsites is about the evenings, and it’s a warning: don’t overbook them. A team that has spent eight hours in meetings doesn’t want a second program at dinner. It wants a good meal, a drink, and something in the space that gives people who have only met on video a reason to stand near each other. That’s the gap this post fills.

Where remote teams go in Colorado #

Colorado wins the offsite for a practical reason before a scenic one: a hub airport a distributed team can reach from most of the country without a connection, and a mountain evening within two hours of it. What the search results for a Colorado company retreat show is venues, and they fall into four kinds.

The guest ranches. Devil’s Thumb Ranch outside Tabernash publishes barns and pavilions from forty to two hundred and fifty people, fly fishing, zip lines, ropes courses and horseback riding for the team-building slots, and nightly s’mores as the evening program. C Lazy U near Granby sells all-inclusive group rates with lodging, meals and ranch activities in one figure. These are the offsites where the whole company sleeps on the property and the evening happens in a barn.

The mountain-town basecamps. Gravity Haus positions its Breckenridge, Vail, Winter Park, Steamboat Springs and Denver locations for, in its own words, “remote board meetings, company retreats, and team bonding,” with coworking space, restaurants and bars and gear rental in one building. This is the offsite for the company that wants the town.

The resort towns. Vail, Beaver Creek, Aspen and Breckenridge, where the hotels run group business all year and the evening comes with a decibel cap and a curfew written into the contract. Our retreats page and its town pages cover the drive, the lodging and the sound rules for each town.

Denver itself. For the company that flies in and stays downtown, the convention-center hotels and the city’s own venues host the working days, and the Colorado evening is a drive away. A growing set of remote-first companies are based here already; “remote companies colorado” is a search Google completes on its own.

The arrival night: an acoustic set under the first dinner #

The first evening of an offsite is the strangest hour of the year for a remote company. Sixty people who know each other’s faces from a grid are in one place, and the first ten minutes decide whether the week feels like a reunion or a conference. An acoustic duo or the Americana trio at conversation volume, 65 to 72 dBA at the nearest table, gives people a reason to drift toward the same corner and something to stand next to while they work out who’s who. Nobody is asked to do anything, and the evening has a middle to it that no icebreaker had to supply.

The Colorado evening #

A third of planners now report more demand for locally flavored events, 34 percent in the most recent industry forecast, and a team that flew in from six cities wants one evening that could only have happened here. The Americana band plays the songs people expect in the mountains, the Grateful Dead, Tom Petty and Neil Young, beside the songs written here, Nathaniel Rateliff and Gregory Alan Isakov, on acoustic guitar, fiddle, upright bass and drums. In a ranch barn it’s the dinner and the dance floor in one booking.

Live band karaoke, the remote team's icebreaker #

Karaoke sits near the top of the offsite ideas lists the big employers publish, and for a remote team it does something a trivia night cannot: it puts the quiet engineer from the Lisbon time zone on a stage with the head of sales, singing Journey, with a real band behind them. The band plays the 80s and 90s hits on its own, sung out front, and hands the mic to anyone who wants a song, with the words on a screen and a host on the floor. Nobody has to sing for the night to work, and by the third song the list fills on its own. It’s the evening people quote back to each other on the video calls for the rest of the year. The live band karaoke page has the songbook and the builds.

The last night: the whole company on one floor #

Most offsites end on the one evening that’s a party without a program. The act is the yacht rock band for a crowd that wants to sing every chorus, the soul and R&B band for a ballroom floor, or the DJ with a live musician over the set, on the sound system that has been in the building since the first night. A band handing off to the DJ through the same system covers a company that wants to keep dancing after the band’s last set, and it’s one contract for the whole stay.

The winter offsite is an apres-ski evening by default #

December-to-March offsites are the growth segment, and a Colorado company that books one gets the theme for free: the snow is outside the window. The evening is the Americana band by the fire, a solo pianist through a long dinner, or the karaoke night in a ski-town bar the company has bought out. The retreats page carries the mountain rules, and the Vail evening ideas post covers the town in detail.

The unstructured evening, with music under it #

The evening the engineers asked for, with no session, no facilitator and no slide. It’s a long table, or several, with a jazz duo or a solo pianist at a level the table talks across. It’s the cheapest idea on this list and the one that most often produces the conversation the offsite was for, because the two people who needed to talk about the roadmap finally do it over a drink with nobody watching.

The founder's fireside #

Many remote companies hold one evening where the founder or the CEO talks without slides, and the format wants music around it the way an award night does: something before, silence during, something after. A solo Spanish guitarist or the acoustic duo plays through the gathering, an announcement microphone covers the remarks, and the music comes back in on the applause. The way the music works around remarks is set out on the galas and award nights page, and it applies to a fireside exactly.

The daytime belongs to the venues and outfitters #

The team-building slots belong to the venues and the outfitters: ropes courses, fly fishing, a cattle-roping clinic, a hike, a cooking class, the s’mores. Two of those across the stay is the average, and none of them is a music vendor’s business. What the music does is hold the evenings together, and it does that best when the day was theirs and the night isn’t another program.

The one exception on our side is Firstwater, a seventy-five-minute sound bath with the whole team lying on the floor and nothing to do. It goes into the agenda to open a morning or close an evening, and nobody has to do anything for it beyond lying down.

Ask whoever is planning your week what the evenings hold, and if the answer is “dinner,” that’s the gap.
